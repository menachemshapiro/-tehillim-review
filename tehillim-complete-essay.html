Tehillim Ohel Yosef Yitzchak: The History of Its Text and Printing

Research draft for review — 23 November 2026 Scope: The compact Kehot edition, the 1953 Yahal Or, repairs to the original printing, subsequent proposals to replace or reset it, and later editions.

Evidence convention: “Documented” means a dated letter or publication record is reproduced or directly identified. “Reported” means a recollection or retrospective account, even when credible. “Unresolved” marks a discrepancy or an assertion for which the primary document has not yet been examined. An author's interpretation of the Rebbe's response is not treated as a verbatim directive.

The story in detail

1. Choosing the original compact Tehillim, 1950–1952

After the passing of the Frierdiker Rebbe in 1950, the Rebbe instructed Rabbi Leibel Groner to bring several Tehillim editions from the synagogue at 770. Groner later recalled that the Rebbe examined them, chose one, and noted that its lettering was clearer and that it was more comfortable to read. But the Rebbe’s later explanation, as reported by Rabbi Schneur Zalman Chanin, gives an additional, central reason for the choice: care was taken to select a Tehillim of the kind from which the Frierdiker Rebbe recited, and that edition was photographically reproduced. Thus the accounts can be read together: legibility was mentioned in the selection story, while continuity with the Frierdiker Rebbe’s Tehillim was explicitly emphasized in later explanations. The selection story suggests that the Rebbe was looking for an edition that would be easy for readers to use while remaining faithful to the version used by the Frierdiker Rebbe. Had the exact edition already been available and suitable, the search through several editions would seem unnecessary. Yet the Rebbe’s later responses, as reported by Chanin, appear to identify the chosen edition with the one used by the Frierdiker Rebbe. This tension between the accounts remains unresolved.

What remains unknown: the title-page identity of that pre-Kehot printing—its original publisher, date and place—and its precise relationship to the edition used by the Frierdiker Rebbe. Sources: Dovkin (Groner’s recollection and 1990 response); Mondshine (Chanin’s later account); Mikdash Melech, vol. 2, p. 444.

2. The distinct 1953 Yahal Or publication

In תשי״ג (1953), Kehot published the larger Tehillim Ohel Yosef Yitzchak HaShalem with Yahal Or and additional commentaries. Dovkin expressly states that the Rebbe selected a different printing source for this volume. Thus, the same commemorative name could appear on Tehillim publications using different sources.

According to the reported reading of Zusha Wolf’s הוצאת ספרים קה״ת, p. 81, the 1953 publication appears to have been a second printing, with the first issued before the passing of the Frierdiker Rebbe. This page has not been independently examined.

Limit of the evidence: We have not collated the original compact text against the 1953 Yahal Or to determine whether its vowels and accents actually differ. The statement is about the source edition, not a documented list of variants. Source: Dovkin.

2a. Direct correspondence about proofreading and bibliographical accuracy, 1969 and 1971

23 Shevat 5729 (1969). In a letter of the Rebbe's secretariat signed by Rabbi Chaim Mordechai Aizik Chodakov, responding to an inquiry about printing Tehillim and Tanya, permission to proceed with printing is accompanied by a specific caution: repeated reproductions had damaged parts of letters and vowel marks in the Tehillim, to the point that readers unfamiliar with the text might not know how to pronounce some words. The letter advises:

כדאי הי' שלפני ההדפסה החדשה יעבור — מי שאפשר לסמוך עליו — על כל התהלים ויתקן את הדרוש בזה.

“Before the new printing, it would be advisable for a trustworthy person to go through the entire Tehillim and correct what requires correction.” This is a dated, directly documented instruction to proofread and repair the existing text, not evidence of permission to substitute a different Masoretic printing. Source: Yemei Temimim, vol. 8, printed p. 434, 23 Shevat 5729.

Figure 6. The 1969 letter signed by Rabbi Chodakov concerning damaged letters and nikud in Tehillim.

4 Iyar 5731 (1971). Rabbi Ephraim Wolf writes to Rabbi Chodakov, commenting on the need to avoid errors and unsuitable photographic reproductions. He cites a Tehillim containing three title pages, each carrying a different printing year: “ולדוגמא בתהלים שיש ג' שערים בכל שער שנת הוצאה אחרת.” This is a publishing-accuracy concern, not a statement by the Rebbe concerning the preferred nikud or ta'amim. Since the passage is part of Wolf's letter, we should not attribute the comment directly to the Rebbe. Source: Yemei Temimim, vol. 5, printed pp. 163–164 (the passage continues from p. 163 onto p. 164).

3. Deterioration, limited corrections and the $21 repair fund — date uncertain

As the photographic pages were repeatedly reproduced, some letters and vowel points became broken or unclear. The Silberstrom Teshurah records efforts to correct individual errors, sometimes directly on the printing master. It gives an example from Psalm 64:2, where the dot on the ש in בשיחי was in the wrong position and was corrected.

It also describes preparations for a larger siddur: because the earlier photographic material was unclear and did not fit the intended layout, an alternative Tehillim source was considered or inserted as a working solution. The account says the Rebbe did not agree to this substitution. This should not be confused with proof that no correction to an individual letter was ever permitted.

In the same account, Rabbi Chaim Mordechai Aizik Chodakov arranged a $21 allocation for pencils, pens, erasers, correction fluid and stickers so that the old plates/pages could be repaired rather than wholly reset.

Date caution: Neither of the supplied Silberstrom pages dates the $21 payment or the larger-siddur incident. Source: Silberstrom–Katan Teshurah, pp. 40–41.

Figure 2. Firsthand account of typographic corrections, the attempted substitution and the $21 repair supplies.

4. The 1990 Rödelheim proposal: a change with textual consequences

On 23 Menachem Av 5750, Chanin submitted an Israeli Tehillim that had recently been typeset and proposed using it as a cleaner photographic source. Crucially, he wrote that it followed דפוס רדלהיים (Rödelheim) and contained several differences in nikud from Kehot's existing Tehillim. He enclosed examples and asked whether the new source could be used despite these differences.

The reported response, delivered orally through R. Leibel Groner, emphasized that the Frierdiker Rebbe had not recited Tehillim from Rödelheim, and that special care had been taken to choose a Tehillim associated with his practice. Berger interprets this as a refusal to change the inherited text. We should distinguish the recorded response from that author's interpretation.

On the Rödelheim evidence: Berger is not the only modern author to reproduce the letter. Dovkin does as well, but both are reproducing the same 1990 exchange, not necessarily independent witnesses. Berger's article. · Dovkin's article.

5. The 5751–5752 requests: a different question about typesetting

Chanin later said that he approached the Rebbe twice more, in 5751 and 5752, to ask about typesetting Ohel Yosef Yitzchak afresh with the same style of letters and page arrangement. This differed materially from the Rödelheim proposal: the proposed new setting was presented as a way of improving legibility while preserving the old visual format, rather than explicitly adopting another Masoretic edition.

A report in Mikdash Melech, vol. 2, p. 444, is explicitly attributed to Chanin's notes dated 28 Nissan 5756. It says that sample pages were submitted and recounts a response emphasizing that the original book was photographed from the type of Tehillim used by the Frierdiker Rebbe, whereas the newly submitted one was not. However, Dovkin reports that Chanin disputed the wording printed in Mikdash Melech and said he had never heard that version.

Figure 3. The shorter account, attributed to Chanin's notes of 28 Nissan 5756.

6. The 1996 letter to Wolf: Chanin's own interpretation

In a 28 Nissan 5756 letter to R. Mordechai Menachem Wolf, as quoted by Rabbi Yehoshua Mondshine, Chanin explains the 5751–5752 requests and why the proposed edition was not printed in New York. He adds that in his understanding the matter was “entirely spiritual.” His final words to Wolf leave Wolf to decide what to do, while Chanin transmits the response he understood himself to have received.

This correspondence closely resembles, rather than simply contradicts, the account printed in Mikdash Melech.

7. Later controversy over a newly typeset edition

By the late 1990s, Otzar Sifrei Lubavitch had published a reset edition under the name Ohel Yosef Yitzchak. Mondshine criticized this publication, arguing from the 1996 Chanin letter that the original form ought to have been preserved. That criticism is Mondshine's position, not an independently verified statement of what the Rebbe had prohibited in every circumstance.

A later bibliographical summary refers to a publisher's clarification in **Beis Moshiach, issue 225, p. 23 (5759), reportedly asserting that a response had been quoted inaccurately and arguing for a narrower reading focused on the biblical text rather than all features of typography. The original notice has not yet been examined**, so the report will not present that explanation as a verified direct quotation. Mondshine's article.

The 2003 exchange in Kfar Chabad

Mondshine's series Eizehu HaSiddur Shel HaRebbe (איזהו הסידור של הרבי) appeared in three installments: issue 1047 (5 Sivan 5763), pp. 46–52; issue 1048 (12 Sivan), pp. 36–42; and issue 1049 (19 Sivan), pp. 38–45. Rabbi Leibel Groner responded in issue 1048, pp. 10–11, and subsequently in issues 1050, pp. 14–15, and 1051, pp. 18–19; these latter page ranges include other contributions to the exchange. Further responses appeared in subsequent issues.

The following extracts concern the broader question of preserving or changing the format of Kehot publications. They are drawn from Mondshine's subsequent polemic against Groner, Kavim Lidmuto Shel Shakran: HaRav Yehudah Leib Groner (קווים לדמותו של שקרן: הרב יהודה לייב גרונר), rather than from the original magazine issues, which we have not examined directly. The claims are presented as reported statements, not as independently authenticated directives from the Rebbe.

Issue 1048 — the Machzor. Groner maintained that the Rebbe himself authorized the explanatory note concerning the omission of sheva-na markings and that every page of the Machzor had been shown to the Rebbe before printing. (Polemic, opening chapter.)

Issues 1048 and 1050 — reprinting Torah Or. Groner reported in issue 1048 that repeated requests to reprint Torah Or received the answer, “עס איז דאך פאראן דער סידור תהילת ה׳” — “After all, there is Siddur Tehillat Hashem.” In issue 1050, he recalled Chodakov responding, “כנראה עדיין לא הגיע הזמן לזה” — “Apparently the time for this has not yet come.” Mondshine regarded these explanations as inconsistent. He also related testimony from R. Elchanan Tzvi Gorman that the Rebbe supported reprinting Torah Or through central Kehot. (Polemic, pp. 6–7.)

Issue 1051 — sheva-na markings. According to Groner's account as summarized by Mondshine, someone proposed adding stars identifying sheva-na to the Machzor, and the Rebbe declined because of concern about errors. Mondshine asked the person who made the proposal to publish a firsthand account, and later reported that nobody had responded. (Polemic, p. 44.)

Sources: Mondshine's bibliography, entry 715 (publication details and responses); the three-part series; the subsequent polemic (PDF), opening chapter, pp. 6–7 and 44.

8. Kehot's 2012 Tehillim: retypesetting and differences in pointing

Two 2012 publication records provide context:

• A Hebrew Siddur Tehillat Hashem with Tehillim, ISBN 9780826600820, is cataloged as a newly typeset siddur; its appended Tehillim is described in the listing as digitally enhanced and corrected.

• A bilingual Ohel Yosef Yitzchak, ISBN 9780826600790, is described as a “New Emended Hebrew Edition, With an English Translation.”

Direct examination by the researcher resolves an important question left open by the catalog descriptions: the 2012 Kehot Tehillim uses newly composed Hebrew type; it is not merely a digitally cleaned-up photographic reproduction of the older setting. The researcher has also found numerous differences in nikud and ta'amim in comparison with an earlier edition. Consequently, this development should not be described simply as cosmetic restoration.

See the illustrated comparison in the appendix for side-by-side photographs documenting differences in nikud and ta’amim between the earlier printing and the 2012 edition.

9. Later reprints and the still-unidentified original

Chabadpedia reports that Kehot's 2021–2022 Tehillim editions reuse photographed text pages from earlier editions, even when the book itself contains newer supplements. That is also consistent with why a late publishing date alone cannot tell us whether an edition retains the older typography.

What the documents show—and what they do not

1. The original choice was deliberate. Groner recalls the practical advantage of clear lettering, while the Rebbe’s later explanation as reported by Chanin expressly emphasizes choosing the kind of Tehillim used by the Frierdiker Rebbe. The latter consideration must not be omitted from the selection account. 2. Repairs and substitution are different. The Silberstrom account describes correcting defective letters while saying that the Rebbe rejected another Tehillim photographic source for a larger siddur. 3. The 1990 and 5751–5752 proposals were different. The first explicitly introduced Rödelheim-based nikud differences; the later ones concerned new typesetting in an old-looking layout. 4. The testimonies overlap but are not completely reconciled. The 1996 Wolf letter and Mikdash Melech share much of their account; Dovkin reports Chanin's objection to Mikdash Melech's wording. 5. Kehot's 2012 Tehillim was retypeset, and pointing differences have been observed. The researcher has directly identified the new type and numerous nikud/ta'amim differences from an earlier printing. A documented verse-by-verse comparison would establish the precise readings and significance of those changes.

Additional lead: Volume 6 still requires verification

The direct scans supplied by the researcher verify vol. 8 p. 434 (1969) and vol. 5 pp. 163–164 (1971), which are now included in the timeline. A separate secondary article in Hiskashrut, issue 1374, cites Yemei Temimim vol. 6, pp. 375 and 378 for short directions about printing corrections to Tehillim and offset copying of the siddur/Tehillim; it also refers to p. 330 in a context about Tanya, which is not a Tehillim-specific source.

These volume-6 references remain unverified. The Hiskashrut article has bibliographic inconsistencies: for example, its reference for the 1969 letter gives a different volume number from the actual page 434 scan supplied here. Moreover, its assignment of a 1970 event to volume 6 is hard to reconcile with the announcement that volume 6 covers 5733–5735 (1973–1975). The chronology will not silently promote those secondary references to confirmed events. Hiskashrut issue 1374; Volume-6 publication announcement.

Purpose and scope

This comparison asks whether the new edition is intended to be a faithful recreation of the old edition, or merely a lookalike based on a completely different textual source. It does not attempt to determine which edition is more accurate or whether any particular change was intentional.

The comparison focuses on two categories of differences: changes in vocalization (nikud) and changes in cantillation (ta’amim). Both demonstrate differences in the underlying text, rather than merely in its visual presentation.

3. Conclusion

The addition and omission of dageshim in the bet, together with the differing vowel configuration at Psalm 32:6, provide evidence of changes beyond the graphical appearance of the printed text. The cantillation comparisons further demonstrate differences, although some recurring changes may reflect alternative conventions for rendering the same sign. The conclusion does not depend on establishing which edition is correct, or on attributing the changes to any particular editorial process.
